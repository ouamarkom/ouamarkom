Official Glossary

Command Economy of AI (CE-AI)
A Conceptual Reference for AI Economic Agency, Governance, Execution, and Evidence

OUAMARKOM™ studies how artificial intelligence systems move from generating information and planning tasks to participating in actions that affect economic resources, transactions, commitments, and decisions.

This glossary defines the concepts used across OUAMARKOM™’s research and engineering work. It distinguishes established technical concepts from working definitions, research hypotheses, and assets that remain under development.

The glossary is a developing conceptual reference, not an accredited technical standard. Its definitions may evolve as experiments, evidence, and independent evaluations refine the underlying research.

Introduction

Understanding AI Economic Action

As AI systems gain access to tools, APIs, enterprise applications, and operational workflows, a set of important distinctions becomes increasingly relevant:

  • Capability: What a system can technically do.
  • Authority and delegation: What the system is permitted to do, within which scope and under whose authorization.
  • Governance: The policies, responsibilities, and controls that regulate permitted actions.
  • Execution: What actually happens in the operational environment.
  • Evidence and verification: What can be substantiated about an action and its execution.
  • Economic outcomes: The observable or measurable effects of an action on resources, costs, revenues, commitments, or economic risk.

The Command Economy of AI (CE-AI) is a research and analytical framework developed by OUAMARKOM™ to organize questions concerning these relationships.

Usage

How to Use This Glossary

Each glossary entry may include:

  1. A definition.
  2. Its research or operational significance.
  3. Its relationship to the CE-AI framework.
  4. A practical example.
  5. Related concepts.
  6. A development-status note where appropriate.

The glossary is intended to support consistent terminology across research papers, experimental protocols, benchmark specifications, engineering documents, and evidence reports.

A term's inclusion does not imply universal agreement on its definition, demonstrated technical necessity, or formal recognition by an external standards body.

The Conceptual Pathway

From Capability to Economic Outcome

The relationships among these concepts do not form a mandatory linear sequence for every AI system. They represent a conceptual map of elements that may interact during AI-enabled economic action.

Capability → Intent or Goal → Command or Task Request → Planning → Authority and Delegation → Execution Controls → Economic Action → Evidence and Verification → Economic Outcome

Depending on the system, the process may involve multiple agents, tools, approval steps, exception handling, or changes in authorization during execution. A system may need to revalidate authority, escalate a decision to a human, stop a workflow, or attempt to reverse an action where reversal remains possible.

Core research principle: Technical capability does not automatically confer authority. A command does not, by itself, establish authorization. Successful technical execution does not independently establish that the intended economic outcome has been achieved.

Concepts 1–7

Core Concepts

1

Command Economy of AI (CE-AI)

Definition

A research and analytical framework developed by OUAMARKOM™ to study economic actions performed by or involving AI systems, including the relationships among commands, capability, authority, delegation, governance, execution, evidence, and economic outcomes.

Why It Matters

CE-AI provides a structured way to investigate the transition from AI-generated information to actions that can affect transactions, resources, contractual commitments, and economic decisions.

Example

Investigating whether existing controls can prevent an AI agent from executing a financial transfer after its authorization has been revoked.

Scope and Status

CE-AI is a developing research framework, not an established technical standard, finished product, or replacement for existing economic systems.

AI Economic Action

Definition

An action performed or facilitated by an AI system that affects, or is intended to affect, economic resources, transactions, commitments, prices, procurement decisions, or other economically relevant activities.

Why It Matters

It shifts the focus from what an AI system can say or recommend to what it actually does and what consequences follow.

Example

Creating a purchase order, submitting a price offer, modifying a contract, or initiating a financial transfer.

Scope and Distinctions

A conversational response is not necessarily an economic action. Research and operational records should distinguish a proposed action, a simulated action, an execution attempt, a completed action, and a verified outcome.

Technical Capability

Definition

The set of technical abilities that enables a system to perform a task, such as analyzing information, planning, using a tool, or invoking an API.

Why It Matters

Capability describes what a system can technically do. It does not independently establish what the system is authorized to do.

Example

An AI agent may be technically able to submit a purchase order through an API, while its permitted spending limit remains restricted.

Related Principle

Capability Does Not Confer Authority.

4

Human Intent

Definition

The goal or desired outcome a person seeks to achieve, as explicitly expressed or inferred from the available context.

Why It Matters

Intent helps establish the purpose of a task, but does not necessarily specify every permitted action or grant authority to execute it.

Example

“I want to reduce procurement costs” describes a goal. It does not automatically authorize an AI system to terminate existing contracts or change suppliers without constraints.

Relationship to Commands

A command may express human intent directly, partially, or ambiguously. The intended goal must therefore be distinguished from the actual instruction and the applicable authorization.

5

Command

Definition

An instruction or task request directed to a system to initiate an activity or pursue a specified objective.

Why It Matters

A command may initiate or direct work, but it does not independently prove that its issuer has the authority to grant permission or that the requested action satisfies all applicable requirements.

Example

“Prepare a transfer request for SAR 5,000 for review” is different from “Execute the transfer.” Neither instruction alone proves that the required financial authorization exists.

Key Distinction

A command is not necessarily authorization. Authorization is not evidence of execution. Execution is not proof of an economic outcome.

6

Intent Understanding

Definition

The process through which a system interprets a user's objective, instructions, context, and relevant constraints to form a representation of the requested task.

Why It Matters

Intent understanding can help identify ambiguity, conflicting requirements, or missing information before planning or execution.

Example

When asked to “select the best supplier,” a system may need to clarify whether best means lowest price, highest quality, shortest delivery time, or the strongest overall fit.

Scope and Distinctions

Understanding intent does not establish the requester's identity, verify their authority, or grant permission to execute the requested action.

Language Interface

Definition

An interaction layer that allows people to communicate with digital systems through natural language rather than relying exclusively on graphical controls or traditional programming interfaces.

Why It Matters

Language interfaces can reduce the need to specify every operational step manually, but may introduce ambiguity or variation in interpretation.

Example

A user describes a procurement objective in natural language, after which the system requests clarification about budget limits and supplier restrictions.

Scope and Distinctions

A language interface is a means of interaction, not an independent authorization or execution-control mechanism.

Concepts 8–15

Economic Agency, Authority, and Governance

8

AI Economic Agency

Definition

The capacity of an AI system, within a particular environment, to participate in actions affecting economic resources, transactions, commitments, or outcomes, with varying degrees of autonomy and delegation.

Why It Matters

It distinguishes systems that only provide recommendations from systems that can initiate or execute operational actions through available tools and permissions.

Example

One AI system recommends a price reduction. Another can directly change a product's price in a sales system if its permissions allow it.

Scope and Distinctions

Economic agency describes a system's participation in economic action. It does not automatically confer independent legal authority or determine legal responsibility.

Related Concepts

9

Authority

Definition

The permission or recognized right granted to an entity or system to make a decision or perform an action within a defined scope and under applicable rules.

Why It Matters

Technical ability to perform an action does not establish that the system is permitted to perform it.

Example

An AI agent may prepare a purchase order while final approval remains the responsibility of an authorized procurement manager.

Related Concepts
  • Delegated Authority
  • Authority Boundary
  • Governance
  • Approval
10

Delegated Authority

Definition

The granting of specified permissions to an agent or system to perform actions or make decisions within defined limits, conditions, and responsibilities.

Why It Matters

Delegation establishes who may perform an action, which actions are permitted, and under what conditions the permission remains valid.

Example

An AI agent may issue purchase orders up to SAR 10,000, while purchases involving new suppliers require additional approval.

Potential Components
  • The person or entity granting authority.
  • The agent or system receiving authority.
  • The permitted actions.
  • Financial or operational limits.
  • Duration and expiration conditions.
  • Approval and escalation requirements.
  • Revocation procedures and enforcement mechanisms.
Related Concepts

11

Authority Boundary

Definition

The limits separating actions an agent is permitted to perform from actions that require additional authorization or are prohibited.

Why It Matters

Authority boundaries translate general permissions into operational constraints that can be examined and tested.

Example

An agent may request supplier quotations but may not approve a purchase that exceeds its authorized budget.

Research Question

Do authority boundaries remain effective across multi-step workflows, changing contexts, tool calls, and delegated subtasks?

Related Concepts

12

Governance

Definition

The policies, responsibilities, decision-making rules, and review mechanisms that regulate system use, permissions, risks, exceptions, and changes.

Why It Matters

Governance connects institutional objectives and policies to operational processes and accountability.

Example

A policy requires human approval for transfers above a specified threshold and records the approver, approval time, and transaction status.

Scope and Distinctions

A written policy does not, by itself, prove that the policy is technically enforced or effective under operational conditions.

Related Concepts

13

Human Oversight

Definition

The practical ability of authorized people to review, approve, reject, or stop system actions within a defined scope of responsibility.

Why It Matters

Effective oversight requires more than a human being present in the workflow. It also requires adequate information, actual decision authority, and a meaningful opportunity to intervene.

Example

Before approving a transfer, a reviewer sees the amount, recipient, authorization source, and relevant transaction details.

Related Concepts
  • Approval

14

Human-in-the-Loop (HITL)

Definition

An operating pattern in which a human participates at one or more points in an AI workflow, such as approving an action, reviewing an output, or handling an exception.

Why It Matters

Human participation can provide an additional review or authorization step before, during, or after an action, depending on the system design.

Example

An AI agent prepares a transfer request but must wait for an authorized employee's approval before submitting it.

Scope and Distinctions

A human approval step does not automatically guarantee safety. The approver's identity and authority must be established, the approval must be linked to the correct transaction, and the decision must be enforced.

Related Concepts
  • Human Oversight
  • Authority
  • Delegation
  • Execution
15

Revocation of Authority

Definition

The cancellation or termination of previously granted permission, such that the affected agent or system is no longer authorized to perform the actions covered by that permission.

Why It Matters

Workflows may continue after a task begins. Research must therefore examine how revocation behaves during planning, waiting, execution, and other operational states.

Example

A financial agent's authorization is revoked after a transfer has been prepared but before it is submitted. The experiment tests whether the transfer is prevented when the authorization is no longer valid.

Research Question

What is the last point in the execution lifecycle at which revocation can be enforced reliably? Which actions, if any, become irreversible beyond that point?

Concepts 16–22

Planning, Orchestration, and Execution

16

Planning

Definition

The process of identifying the actions, resources, dependencies, and conditions required to pursue an objective.

Why It Matters

Planning translates a goal into steps that can be evaluated, reviewed, and potentially executed.

Example

Breaking a procurement task into requirements definition, quotation collection, supplier comparison, approval, and purchase-order submission.

Scope and Distinctions

A plan is not an execution. It does not establish that every planned action is authorized or feasible under current conditions.

Related Concepts

17

AI Agent

Definition

A system that uses AI capabilities to pursue an objective or perform a task and may incorporate planning, tool use, and interactions with other systems.

Why It Matters

Governance requirements depend partly on what an agent can access and what actions it can actually perform.

Example

One agent reads inventory data and recommends replenishment. Another can issue purchase orders directly.

Scope and Distinctions

Describing a system as an agent does not imply complete autonomy or unlimited authority.

Related Concepts

18

Agent Orchestration

Definition

The coordination of interactions, dependencies, and information flows among multiple agents, tools, or services to accomplish a shared task.

Why It Matters

Additional risks may arise when tasks pass between components or when one agent delegates work to another.

Example

One agent collects quotations, another compares them, an enterprise service checks the budget, and an authorized employee approves the purchase.

Research Question

How are permissions, approvals, and evidence records preserved as tasks move across components?

Workflow

Definition

A structured set of connected steps and transition rules that determine how a task progresses from one state to another.

Why It Matters

Workflows identify decision points, approval gates, stopping conditions, retries, and escalation paths.

Example

Purchase request created → budget reviewed → request approved → order submitted to supplier → confirmation recorded.

Related Concepts
  • Planning
  • Agent Orchestration

  • Verification
20

Execution

Definition

The performance of an action or sequence of actions within an operational environment according to specified instructions and conditions.

Why It Matters

Execution marks the transition from a plan or request to an action that has occurred or has been attempted.

Example

Submitting a purchase order to an enterprise system through an API.

Scope and Distinctions

A request to execute, an execution attempt, system acceptance, completed execution, and a verified economic outcome are different states and should be recorded separately.

Related Concepts
  • Execution Control

  • Verification
  • Outcome
21

Execution Control

Definition

The mechanisms used to permit, prevent, suspend, or revalidate operational actions according to applicable permissions, policies, and conditions.

Why It Matters

Permissions and circumstances may change between task creation and execution. An authorization check performed only at the beginning may therefore be insufficient in some workflows.

Example

Rechecking a transfer's authorization at the point of submission rather than relying exclusively on an earlier check.

Scope and Distinctions

No single control mechanism should be assumed appropriate for every system. Proposed controls should be compared with existing safeguards and simpler alternatives.

Related Concepts
  • Delegated Authority
  • Authority Boundary
  • Revocation
  • Governed Execution
22

Governed Execution

Definition

The execution of actions within applicable authority limits, policies, approval requirements, and operational constraints, with the ability to evaluate compliance using appropriate evidence.

Why It Matters

Governed execution connects what a system is permitted to do with what actually happens during operation.

Example

A financial transfer is blocked when its authorization has expired or a required approval is missing.

Scope and Distinctions

The term does not imply that execution is risk-free or that outcomes are guaranteed. The effectiveness of controls must be demonstrated within a defined evaluation scope.

Concepts 23–31

Evidence, Verification, and Economic Outcomes

23

Event Log

Definition

A structured record of relevant operational events, including timestamps, identifiers, states, and data needed to reconstruct an execution sequence.

Why It Matters

Event logs support analysis of what happened and comparison of actual behavior against specified conditions.

Example

Recording when authority was granted, when it was revoked, when authorization was checked, when execution was attempted, and what response followed.

Related Concepts
  • Evidence
  • Verification
  • Experiment Log
  • Economic Exposure
24

Execution Evidence

Definition

Data, records, and references that support a specific claim about an action or operational state.

Why It Matters

Evidence helps reviewers assess whether an action occurred, under which conditions, and whether a conclusion is supported by the available records.

Example

An internal log stating that a transfer completed may be insufficient if the claim requires confirmation from the financial system that processed the transaction.

Scope and Distinctions

A record is not automatically reliable evidence. Its source, completeness, timing, integrity, and connection to the claimed event must be evaluated.

Related Concepts

  • Economic Outcome
25

Verification

Definition

The process of examining evidence to determine whether a claim, action, or output satisfies specified conditions and criteria.

Why It Matters

Verification distinguishes what a system reports from what can be substantiated using appropriate data.

Example

Checking that a purchase order remained within its authorized limit and that the required approval occurred before submission.

Scope and Distinctions

Verifying that an action occurred does not necessarily establish that it achieved the intended economic result. Economic outcomes may require separate measurement.

Related Concepts
  • Execution Evidence
  • Outcome
  • Evidence Package
  • Event Log
26

Outcome

Definition

The state or change actually achieved following an action or sequence of actions, compared with a desired or reference state.

Why It Matters

An outcome helps establish whether a process achieved its intended result, rather than merely completing its operational steps.

Example

A supplier's acceptance of a purchase order is an operational state. Receiving the agreed goods in the required quantity and quality is a subsequent outcome that requires separate verification.

Related Concepts
  • Execution
  • Verification
  • Economic Outcome
27

Economic Outcome

Definition

An observable or measurable economic effect associated with an action or decision, such as a change in cost, revenue, resource utilization, financial commitment, or risk exposure.

Why It Matters

Economic outcomes help determine whether an action created practical value and whether that value justifies the cost and risk of execution and control.

Example

Reducing procurement costs while maintaining agreed quality and contractual requirements.

Scope and Distinctions

An observed improvement does not automatically prove that AI caused it. Attribution may require a suitable comparison, control condition, or evaluation design.

Related Concepts
  • Outcome
  • Verification
  • Economic Exposure
  • Economic Value
28

Economic Exposure

Definition

The potential financial or economic value that could be affected by an action, failure, or decision under a specified scenario and set of assumptions.

Why It Matters

Economic exposure describes the scale of a potential risk even when no actual loss has occurred.

Example

An unauthorized transfer request for SAR 5,000 may represent potential exposure of that amount under a defined scenario. It should not be recorded as a realized loss unless a loss actually occurs.

Scope and Distinctions

Potential exposure, realized loss, transaction value, and expected loss are different concepts. Reports should identify which measure is being used and state relevant assumptions.

Evidence Package

Definition

An organized collection of experimental materials needed to review a result, such as the test description, system version, raw data, metric definitions, calculations, limitations, and decision record.

Why It Matters

Evidence packages facilitate review, reanalysis, and replication while reducing reliance on unsupported summaries.

Example

A package containing an experiment identifier, agent configuration, authority limits, event sequence, metric results, and the data supporting the conclusion.

30

Reproducibility

Definition

The ability to repeat an experiment or reanalyze its data using documented procedures and materials to assess whether its results can be obtained under the specified conditions.

Why It Matters

Reproducibility reduces dependence on a single experiment and helps determine whether a result is stable or specific to particular conditions.

Example

Repeating a revocation test under the same settings, then changing one variable to examine its effect.

Scope and Distinctions

Reproducing a result in one environment does not establish that it generalizes to every model, institution, or operational setting.

Related Concepts

31

Decision Memory

Definition

A structured record of previous decisions, their rationale, supporting evidence, observed outcomes, limitations, and lessons learned.

Why It Matters

Decision memory supports traceability, helps prevent repeated mistakes, and documents why a research hypothesis or engineering direction changed.

Example

Recording a decision not to build a new component because existing controls achieved the required performance within the tested conditions.

Scope and Distinctions

Preserving a decision does not establish that it was correct or that a system should follow it automatically. Its context and conditions of validity must remain available.

CEP™

Command Execution Protocol

Definition

An execution protocol under development within OUAMARKOM™'s research program. It is intended to explore how commands, delegated authority, governance controls, and execution boundaries can be connected during operation.

Engineering Question

Can a defined mechanism improve the enforcement of authority limits, the response to changes in authorization, or the quality of execution evidence compared with existing controls and simpler alternatives?

Testing an authorization revalidation mechanism at the execution boundary and comparing failure rates, response times, and operational costs against an appropriate baseline.

Status

A protocol under development, not an established standard or a solution whose necessity has already been demonstrated. Its value must be determined through comparative testing.

Execution infrastructure supports the transition from generating outputs to interacting with systems that may be affected by AI-enabled decisions and actions.

The presence of APIs or execution tools does not, by itself, establish that governance is adequate or that new infrastructure is necessary.

Architecture

The organization of a system's components, interfaces, responsibilities, information flows, and constraints.

Why It Matters

Architecture helps determine where controls should operate and how permissions, events, and evidence should flow between components.

Example

Defining where authorization is checked during a transaction, which component must reject an invalid request, and which source establishes the final transaction status.

Concepts 38–44

Supporting Concepts

38

Metrics

Definition

Quantitative or qualitative indicators used to evaluate a specific aspect of system behavior, control performance, or experimental outcomes.

Example

The rate of unauthorized execution attempts, the response time following authorization revocation, or the proportion of experiments with complete evidence records.

Measurement Principle

Each metric should have a defined calculation method, data source, scope, and limitations before its results are interpreted. Distinct metrics should not be combined into a single score without evidence that such aggregation is valid.

39

Baseline

Definition

A reference condition against which an intervention or change is evaluated, such as the performance of existing controls before a new mechanism is introduced.

Example

Comparing the failure rate before and after adding an authorization check under equivalent test conditions.

Why It Matters

Without an appropriate baseline, it is difficult to determine whether an intervention actually improved performance.

40

Controlled Experiment

Definition

An experiment in which the scenario, variables, conditions, and measurement methods are specified in advance, with relevant factors controlled as far as practical.

Example

Changing the timing of an authorization check while keeping other test conditions constant to examine its effect.

41

Generalizability

Definition

The extent to which findings from an experiment may apply to systems, conditions, or institutions beyond those directly tested.

Example

A control that succeeds in one simulated environment is not automatically proven effective in different financial systems.

Principle

Generalizability must be established through appropriate repetition, varied test conditions, and supporting evidence. It should not be assumed from a single successful experiment.

42

Engineering Intervention

Definition

A technical or operational change introduced to address a measured failure or improve a specified outcome.

Example

Adding an authorization check at the execution boundary or changing how a workflow stops after authority is revoked.

Evaluation Principle

An intervention should be compared with a baseline and reasonable alternatives, measuring its benefits, costs, and side effects.

43

Economic Value

Definition

The benefit generated by a solution or action relative to its costs, risks, and alternatives within a defined context.

Example

Reducing exception-handling costs, lowering financial exposure, or shortening review time, provided these effects can be measured and substantiated.

Scope and Distinctions

Technical sophistication alone does not establish economic value. A solution must provide meaningful benefits to a user or institution relative to available alternatives.

44

Evidence Before Architecture

Definition

A research principle requiring problems, alternatives, and supporting evidence to be examined before committing to a substantial architectural design or broad infrastructure investment.

Application at OUAMARKOM

An experiment begins with a specific research question, a baseline, and defined metrics. The findings then inform whether a new intervention or reusable component is warranted.

Purpose

To reduce the risk of investing prematurely in solutions whose necessity has not been demonstrated.

Interpretation

Principles for Interpreting the Glossary

OUAMARKOM™ uses the following principles when applying these terms in research papers, reports, and technical materials.

  1. Capability does not confer authority. The ability to perform an action does not establish permission to perform it.
  2. A command is not necessarily authorization. An instruction alone does not prove that its issuer can grant the required authority.
  3. Authorization may change. A previously valid delegation may expire or be revoked during a workflow.
  4. Execution is not outcome. Technical completion does not independently establish that the intended economic result was achieved.
  5. A record is not automatically sufficient evidence. Data sources, completeness, integrity, and reliability must be assessed.
  6. One experiment does not establish generalizability. Broader conclusions require appropriate replication and evidence.
  7. A concept is not an accredited standard. Claims of universal adoption, formal approval, or external certification require substantiation.
  8. A hypothesis is not a finding. Research assets and methods under development must be described according to their actual status.
  9. A proposed solution does not establish the need for new infrastructure. Existing controls and simpler alternatives should be evaluated first.
  10. Economic value requires measurement. Observed benefits must be distinguished from estimates, assumptions, and unverified claims.

Glossary Governance and Versioning

This glossary is maintained as a developing conceptual reference within OUAMARKOM™'s research and engineering program.

Definitions may be revised when hypotheses change, new evidence becomes available, or more precise terminology is established.

Where practical, each revision should document:

  • The English and Arabic term.
  • A concise, stable definition.
  • Scope and exclusions.
  • Relationships to other concepts.
  • Relevant research or engineering references.
  • Version number and revision date.
  • Concept status: general concept, working definition, research hypothesis, or asset under development.

Inclusion in the glossary does not imply that a concept is universally accepted or that an associated solution has demonstrated effectiveness. Claims concerning performance, engineering necessity, or economic value remain subject to appropriate evidence.

Methodological principle: Research first. Evidence second. Architecture third. Infrastructure only if justified by the findings.
