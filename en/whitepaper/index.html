Executive and Research Abstract

Abstract

Artificial intelligence is undergoing a rapid transition from systems primarily designed to process information and generate responses toward systems capable of proposing and executing actions through tools, platforms, and digital environments, including actions with economic, operational, and institutional consequences.

This transition raises a fundamental research question:

How can an AI system's ability to propose an economic action be translated into the authority to execute that action under explicitly delegated permissions, enforceable boundaries, independent control mechanisms, and verifiable outcomes?

This paper begins with the hypothesis that the ability to reason, plan, or use tools does not necessarily imply possession of the authority required to execute economic actions. Similarly, granting a system technical access to a tool or application programming interface does not, by itself, establish that the resulting action was properly authorized, permitted, or executed within the required constraints.

The paper proposes a foundational research framework named Command Economy of AI (CE-AI™) to examine the relationship between commands, delegated authority, governed execution, and verifiable outcomes.

The framework is organized around a four-stage conceptual sequence:

Commands → Delegated Authority → Governed Execution → Verifiable Outcomes

The paper also introduces an operational definition of the Integration Gap, referring to the absence, insufficiency, or lack of demonstrated effective coordination among mechanisms that connect AI agents' proposed economic actions to explicit authority delegation, deterministic policy evaluation, controlled execution, action lifecycle management, and independent outcome verification.

Furthermore, the paper examines the relationship between CE-AI and existing research fields, introduces preliminary theoretical propositions and hypotheses, and proposes an engineering architecture and experimental research program involving the Smart Hand™ experimental environment, the CEP™ engineering specification, GEAB experimental scenarios, and UEARM mechanisms for execution validation and evidence recording.

This paper does not claim that the proposed framework has become an adopted standard or an established scientific theory. Nor does it present experimental results that have not yet been produced. Its purpose is to establish a research program that can be examined, challenged, tested, and further developed.

Keywords: Command Economy of AI, Governed AI Agency, Delegated Authority, AI Agents, Execution Control, Integration Gap, Deterministic Execution, Outcome Verification, Agent Governance.

Chapter 1 | Research Motivation

1.1 The Transition from Response to Action

Artificial intelligence systems have evolved from models that primarily provide answers and recommendations toward systems capable of interacting with digital environments, using tools, and executing sequences of operations to achieve defined objectives.

This development creates opportunities for AI agents in areas such as:

  • Procurement and supplier management.
  • Resource and budget allocation.
  • Financial transactions and transfers.
  • Commercial operations management.
  • Operational procedure execution.
  • Interaction with enterprise systems.
  • Management of tasks involving multiple decisions and actions.

However, the transition from response generation to action execution changes the nature of the associated risks.

In a response-oriented system, an inaccurate output may primarily represent a cognitive or informational problem.

In a system capable of executing economic actions, inaccurate or unauthorized outputs may become financial commitments, actual transactions, or changes to the state of institutional systems.

This creates a need to examine the architecture that separates an AI system's ability to make decisions from the authority required to execute those decisions.

1.2 The Core Research Problem

The research problem is not limited to an AI system's ability to generate a plan or recommend an action.

It concerns how to ensure that every economic action executed by an AI system:

  • Is based on a defined and understandable command.
  • Is associated with authority delegated by an authorized entity.
  • Is subject to enforceable policies and boundaries.
  • Passes through a governed execution mechanism.
  • Is subject to clearly defined execution states and lifecycle management.
  • Produces an evidence record that can be examined.
  • Can be independently assessed to determine what was actually executed, rather than relying solely on the system's own claim that the action was completed.

The central issue concerns the relationship among these elements, rather than the isolated existence of each element.

1.3 Central Research Question

How can mechanisms be designed and engineered to separate AI agents' reasoning processes from their delegated economic authority, so that resulting economic actions become controllable, enforceable, traceable, and verifiable?

This question gives rise to the following subsidiary research questions:

  • How can an economic command be represented in a form suitable for interpretation and execution?
  • How can a command be linked to explicitly delegated authority with a defined scope?
  • How can the validity of an action be evaluated deterministically before execution?
  • How can violations of delegated authority boundaries be prevented during execution?
  • How can delegated authority be revoked or suspended during the action lifecycle?
  • How can the system establish what was actually executed and what was not?
  • How can successful process execution be distinguished from achievement of the intended economic outcome?

Chapter 2 | Definition of the CE-AI™ Framework

2.1 Definition

Command Economy of AI (CE-AI™) is a proposed research framework for studying and engineering the relationship between commands directed to AI systems, the economic authority delegated to those systems, governed execution mechanisms, and verifiable outcomes.

Its purpose is to investigate models and mechanisms through which economic actions performed by AI systems can remain within the boundaries of explicit delegation, enforceable rules, and examinable evidence.

The term "Economy" in this framework does not refer to macroeconomics or a political or traditional economic system for resource distribution.

Instead, it refers to the organization of the relationship between commands, authority, resources, actions, and outcomes in contexts where artificial intelligence systems perform activities with economic consequences.

2.2 Research Scope

CE-AI focuses on five interconnected questions:

  • Who has the authority to issue the command?
  • Who has the authority to delegate execution permissions?
  • What boundaries govern the permitted action?
  • How is the action executed, monitored, and interrupted?
  • How can the outcome be established and verified?

The framework treats these questions as interconnected parts of a single system rather than as isolated technical functions.

2.3 Foundational Sequence of the Framework

The CE-AI™ Core Sequence:

Stage Operational Description
1. Commands Definition of the requested action, its context, and its scope.
2. Delegated Authority Identification of the authorized entity, permission boundaries, and delegation conditions.
3. Governed Execution Policy evaluation, enforcement of constraints, and control of the execution lifecycle.
4. Verifiable Outcomes Evidence recording, state assessment, and verification of the actual outcome.

This sequence represents the framework's foundational conceptual structure. It does not imply that all existing systems lack these stages or that their integration has never been studied.

2.4 Distinctions Between the Stages

The four stages must not be treated as interchangeable concepts:

  • A command does not constitute delegation.
  • Delegation does not automatically authorize every action.
  • Authorization does not establish that execution occurred.
  • Execution does not establish that the intended outcome was achieved.
  • Recording an event does not constitute independent verification.

Maintaining these distinctions is one of the conceptual foundations of the CE-AI research program.

Chapter 3 | The Central Hypothesis: The Integration Gap

3.1 Definition of the Integration Gap

For the purposes of the CE-AI™ framework, the Integration Gap is operationally defined as:

«The absence, insufficiency, or lack of demonstrated effective coordination among enforceable mechanisms that connect economic actions proposed by AI agents to explicit authority delegation, deterministic policy evaluation, governed transaction execution, action lifecycle management, and independent outcome verification.»

This definition is intended to identify a research problem that can be analyzed and measured. It does not presume that every existing system necessarily exhibits this gap.

3.2 Why Are Isolated Capabilities Insufficient?

A system may contain multiple mechanisms, including:

  • Authentication and identity management.
  • Permission management.
  • Access policies.
  • Execution monitoring tools.
  • Event logs.
  • Audit mechanisms.
  • Transaction application programming interfaces.

However, the existence of these components does not, by itself, demonstrate that they operate in a coordinated manner when an AI agent executes an economic action.

The research question is therefore not simply:

Does an authentication mechanism exist?

It is:

Are the agent's identity, command, authority, action boundaries, execution record, and outcome connected through an integrated process that can be tested?

3.3 The Seven Dimensions of the Integration Gap

  • Command Gap: Insufficient definition of the requested action, its context, or its boundaries.
  • Authority Gap: Unclear delegation, source of authority, permission scope, or authorization validity period.
  • Policy Enforcement Gap: Lack of demonstrated deterministic application of rules and constraints before an action is permitted.
  • Execution Control Gap: Insufficient control over the execution process and inadequate prevention of actions exceeding approved boundaries.
  • Lifecycle Gap: Insufficient management of action states, including suspension, cancellation, expiration, and retry operations.
  • Evidence Gap: Insufficient recording of evidence establishing what actually happened, when it happened, and under which authority.
  • Outcome Verification Gap: Absence of sufficient independent verification of the final state and actual outcome.

These dimensions represent a proposed research classification. Their relative independence, boundaries, and potential overlap must be examined through literature review and experimentation.

Chapter 4 | Foundational Conceptual Distinctions

CE-AI is based on a set of conceptual distinctions intended to prevent confusion between technical capability and operational authority.

First Concept Second Concept Distinction
Capability Authority The ability to perform an action does not grant the right to perform it.
Command Delegation A request to perform an action does not necessarily grant permission to execute it.
Delegation Permission Delegation identifies the source and scope of authority, while policies determine the conditions under which an action is permitted.
Authorization Execution Approval of an action does not establish that the action occurred.
Execution Outcome Completion of an operation does not necessarily establish achievement of the intended economic objective.
Logging Evidence An event record alone is insufficient to establish the accuracy of every reported fact.
Evidence Verification The existence of evidence does not establish that the outcome has been independently verified.

4.1 Capability Is Not Authority

An AI agent may possess the technical capability to invoke a financial API or submit a purchase request.

However, this capability does not establish that the agent possesses valid authorization to perform the transaction.

4.2 Delegation Is Not Execution

An agent may receive a defined permission without every request it subsequently submits being automatically accepted.

Each action must be evaluated against the scope of delegation, applicable policies, and operational conditions.

4.3 Execution Is Not Outcome

A system may successfully submit a transfer instruction. However, this does not establish that the funds reached the intended recipient or that the purpose of the transaction was achieved.

It is therefore necessary to distinguish successful tool invocation from achievement of the intended economic outcome.

4.4 Evidence Is Not Verification

A system may record that a transaction was completed.

Verification, however, requires comparing that claim with appropriate evidence from a trustworthy source, according to the required level of assurance.

Chapter 5 | Research Principles & Methodology

The CE-AI research program is based on the following principles:

  • 5.1 Evidence Before Architecture: An engineering layer should not be established as an independent necessity before identifying the problem it addresses and the evidence supporting its existence.
  • 5.2 No Layer Without Evidence: Every proposed layer or component should have a defined role that can be evaluated experimentally.
  • 5.3 No Independent Layer Without Independent Value: Architectural separation alone is insufficient. The separation must demonstrate measurable operational, security, or research value.
  • 5.4 Every Layer Must Earn Its Existence: Each component should be evaluated against simpler alternatives, including conventional access control, delegation, and policy-based solutions.
  • 5.5 Separation of Reasoning and Execution: The system proposing an action should not be the sole authority responsible for determining whether the action is permitted or for establishing that it succeeded.
  • 5.6 Traceability and Accountability: Every economic action should have a traceable path connecting the command, source of authority, policy decision, execution event, supporting evidence, and outcome.

Chapter 6 | Preliminary Theoretical Propositions & Hypotheses

This paper introduces six preliminary hypotheses that can be examined through experimental research. These hypotheses are not established findings. They represent initial propositions for a structured research program.

  • P1 - Capability–Authority Separation Hypothesis: Separating an AI agent's ability to propose an action from the mechanism that grants economic authority may reduce the likelihood of unauthorized actions, compared with designs that rely on broad execution permissions within the agent environment.
  • P2 - Deterministic Policy Evaluation Hypothesis: Using an independent and deterministic policy evaluation engine may improve the predictability of admission, rejection, and escalation decisions compared with relying solely on language-model decisions.
  • P3 - Out-of-Band Control Hypothesis: An authority control mechanism operating independently of the agent's reasoning cycle may enable constraints to be enforced or execution paths to be interrupted even when the agent continues generating requests or attempting new execution operations.
  • P4 - Delegation Lifecycle Management Hypothesis: Linking delegated authority to its temporal and operational state may improve the handling of expiration, revocation, suspension, and retry operations.
  • P5 - Independent Verification Hypothesis: Separating outcome verification from the agent's own claims and self-generated records may improve the reliability of evidence establishing the final outcome.
  • P6 - Integration Hypothesis: Coordinating commands, authority, policies, execution, evidence, and verification within an integrated process may improve operational governance indicators compared with the use of isolated and uncoordinated mechanisms.

These hypotheses require explicit definitions of independent and dependent variables, appropriate comparative baselines, and reproducible experiments before conclusions can be drawn.

Chapter 7 | Relationship to Existing Research Fields

CE-AI does not present itself as a replacement for existing research fields, nor does it assume that all of its individual components are novel. Instead, it proposes examining their integration in the context of economic actions performed by artificial intelligence systems.

Relevant research fields include:

  • AI Governance: Policies, responsibilities, oversight, and risk management.
  • Information Security: Protection of systems, data, interfaces, and operations.
  • Identity and Access Management: Identity definition and permission administration.
  • Authenticated Delegation: Transfer of permissions within defined identity and delegation contexts.
  • Access Control: Evaluation of whether an actor is authorized to perform a particular action.
  • Distributed Transaction Systems: Consistency management, duplication handling, and failure-state management.
  • Autonomous and Agentic Systems: Agent planning, tool use, and interaction with operational environments.
  • Accounting and Auditing: Recording events and evidence and reviewing transactions.
  • Verifiable Systems: Establishing properties of systems, processes, and outcomes through appropriate verification mechanisms.

7.1 Potential Research Contribution

The novelty claim should not be based on the assumption that delegation, access control, execution logs, or agent governance have never been studied.

Rather, the potential research contribution lies in examining the integrated relationship among these mechanisms within a connected economic execution process, and in translating that relationship into an operational model that can be tested, compared, and measured.

Determining the actual degree of novelty requires a systematic literature review and analysis of relevant prior research, technical specifications, standards, patents, and engineering implementations.

Chapter 8 | Proposed Engineering & Architectural Implications

8.1 From Research Framework to Architecture

CE-AI provides a research framework, but it does not prescribe a single final architecture. Engineering decisions should emerge from research findings and experimental evidence.

Nevertheless, the framework allows the investigation of an architecture containing functional components such as:

  • Command intake interface.
  • Action context definition module.
  • Delegation and authority registry.
  • Policy evaluation engine.
  • Execution control module.
  • Execution state machine.
  • Revocation and suspension mechanism.
  • Evidence recorder.
  • Outcome verification module.

These components are proposed engineering concepts that may be modified. They do not represent a mandatory architecture or evidence that all components have been implemented.

8.2 Separation Between Reasoning and Execution

The paper explores the possibility of placing a control mechanism independently of the agent's reasoning environment.

The objective is to prevent the agent from bypassing or overriding its constraints merely because it possesses the ability to issue an execution request.

Under this approach, the agent may propose an action, while an independent control mechanism evaluates whether the action is authorized before passing it to the execution system.

8.3 Decision Mechanism

A three-outcome operational decision model may be investigated:

  • ADMIT - Admission: Accept the request when all applicable delegation and policy conditions are satisfied.
  • REJECT - Rejection: Reject the request when it violates an explicit constraint or exceeds the boundaries of delegated authority.
  • ESCALATE - Escalation: Refer the request for additional review, human approval, or institutional authorization when decision conditions are incomplete or additional authority is required.

Escalation must not be interpreted as implicit approval. The decision outcome should also be traceable and explainable.

Chapter 9 | Engineering Research & Development Program

This program proposes a set of research and engineering deliverables that can be developed and tested incrementally.

9.1 CEP™ - Command Execution Protocol

CEP is a proposed engineering specification intended to structure the transition of economic commands from the request stage to governed execution.

Potential areas of specification include:

  • Command structure and identifiers.
  • Command source identity.
  • Delegation context.
  • Financial or resource limits.
  • Authorization validity period.
  • Execution admission conditions.
  • Rejection and escalation states.
  • Delegation revocation.
  • Event and evidence recording.

CEP is not presented at this stage as an approved international standard or an industry-adopted protocol.

9.2 Smart Hand™ - Governed Economic Action Experimental Environment

Smart Hand is a proposed experimental environment for studying economic actions executed within defined delegation boundaries and policy constraints.

It is intended to provide a controlled environment in which the following can be tested:

  • Delegation boundaries.
  • Budget compliance.
  • Approved vendor lists.
  • Command expiration.
  • Duplicate prevention.
  • Revocation during execution.
  • Concurrent request handling.
  • Evidence recording.
  • Outcome verification.

Smart Hand should not be described as a completed product or production platform before such status has been demonstrated.

9.3 GEAB - Governed Economic Action Benchmarks

GEAB is a proposed experimental scenario and benchmark program for examining the behavior of governed execution systems under different conditions.

Scenario Domain Research Problem
GEAB-01 Procurement Enforcement of vendor, budget, and purchasing conditions.
GEAB-02 Financial Transfers Verification of delegation, transaction limits, and transaction rules.
GEAB-03 Delegation Revocation Measuring the system's ability to prevent execution after authority has been revoked.

9.4 UEARM - Validation and Evidence Recording Engine

UEARM is a proposed reference implementation concept combining:

  • Validator: Request validation mechanism.
  • Governance Engine: Governance and policy evaluation engine.
  • Execution State Machine: Execution state management.
  • Evidence Recorder: Evidence recording mechanism.

The purpose is to provide an engineering foundation that can be tested and compared, while documenting the limitations and test results of each component.

Chapter 10 | Experimental Research Agenda

10.1 Objective

The objective is to move from theoretical hypotheses to practical experiments that measure whether governed execution mechanisms can reduce unauthorized actions and improve execution reliability and outcome verifiability.

10.2 Comparative Experimental Design

The paper proposes comparing three operational environments:

  • Environment A - Agent-Controlled Execution: The agent possesses execution permissions within a conventional experimental environment, with existing controls documented rather than assumed to be absent.
  • Environment B - Conventional Delegation and Control System: Established identity verification, authorization, and policy mechanisms are used before an action is executed.
  • Environment C - CE-AI-Informed Design: An integrated structure is introduced to coordinate commands, authority, policy evaluation, execution, lifecycle management, evidence, and verification.

The comparison must be designed fairly so that any observed value cannot be attributed to unrelated or unequal environmental characteristics.

10.3 Proposed Evaluation Metrics

Metric What It Measures
Unauthorized Action Rate Rate of actions performed outside valid authorization.
Policy Enforcement Accuracy Accuracy of policy application.
False Admission Rate Rate at which unauthorized actions are incorrectly admitted.
False Rejection Rate Rate at which authorized actions are incorrectly rejected.
Revocation Enforcement Latency Time required to enforce revoked authority.
Execution Exposure Degree of operational exposure during execution.
Evidence Coverage Extent to which required events are supported by evidence.
State Transition Integrity Integrity of transitions between execution states.
Duplicate Execution Rate Rate of repeated or duplicated execution.
Concurrent Request Integrity Integrity of policy enforcement under concurrent requests.
Recovery Success Rate Success rate of recovery from failure conditions.
Outcome Verification Accuracy Accuracy of outcome verification.

These are proposed metrics, not achieved performance results. The calculation method, data sources, and test conditions for each metric must be defined before comparative testing begins.

10.4 Delegation Revocation Experiment

Revocation deserves specific investigation because revoking authorization does not necessarily reverse the effects of an external transaction that has already been accepted or settled.

The research should therefore distinguish between:

  • Revocation before execution begins.
  • Revocation during the preparation stage.
  • Revocation after request admission but before final commitment.
  • Revocation after the action has been executed but before its outcome is confirmed.
  • Revocation after the external transaction has been completed.

The experiment should identify the last operational or temporal boundary at which the action can still be prevented through an enforceable mechanism. It should also measure whether the revocation event reaches all relevant execution components.

10.5 Reproducibility

Each experimental package should include:

  • Environment description and component versions.
  • Test configuration.
  • Test data or data-generation methodology.
  • Scenarios and edge cases.
  • Event logs.
  • Raw results.
  • Metric calculation methodology.
  • Report files.
  • Digital file hashes where appropriate.
  • Instructions for reproducing the experiment.

The objective is to enable another researcher to examine the results and repeat the experiment under documented conditions.

Chapter 11 | Validation Requirements & Research Limitations

11.1 Validation Requirements

Building an engineering prototype is not sufficient to establish the validity of a research hypothesis. Validation requires:

  • An operational definition for every evaluated property.
  • Experiments covering both successful and failure conditions.
  • Tests involving unauthorized requests.
  • Tests involving concurrent and repeated requests.
  • Expiration and revocation tests.
  • Comparisons against appropriate baseline solutions.
  • Analysis of failure cases.
  • Documented quantitative measurements.
  • Independent review where feasible.

11.2 Research Limitations

This paper acknowledges the following limitations:

  • CE-AI is a proposed research framework, not an established scientific theory.
  • The seven Integration Gap dimensions represent a preliminary classification requiring review and refinement.
  • The proposed engineering components are concepts and designs; their inclusion does not establish that they have been fully developed.
  • The proposed metrics require precise definitions and measurement procedures within the research program.
  • Comparisons with existing solutions are necessary to establish additional value.
  • Global priority cannot be inferred from the formulation or publication of this framework.
  • The proposed architecture does not guarantee the prevention of all forms of failure or misuse.

Chapter 12 | Ethical & Governance Considerations

Enabling AI systems to execute actions with economic consequences requires consideration of accountability, transparency, and the protection of affected parties.

Key considerations include:

  • Clarity regarding the entity that granted authority.
  • Prevention of actions exceeding delegated boundaries.
  • Protection of personal data and financial information.
  • Prevention and management of conflicts of interest.
  • Availability of escalation and human review mechanisms.
  • Preservation of evidence integrity and confidentiality.
  • Clear distinctions between the responsibilities of the system, the operating organization, and the delegating authority.
  • Prevention of the use of automation to obscure the source of a decision or responsibility for its consequences.

Future applications must comply with the legal and regulatory requirements applicable to each jurisdiction and economic sector.

Chapter 13 | Potential Research Contributions

The CE-AI program seeks to investigate several potential contributions:

  • 13.1 Conceptual Contribution: Developing a structured framework for studying the relationship between commands, economic authority, governed execution, and verifiable outcomes.
  • 13.2 Analytical Contribution: Developing an operational definition of the Integration Gap and its dimensions, then evaluating the validity of this classification through literature analysis and experimentation.
  • 13.3 Engineering Contribution: Exploring designs that separate agent reasoning from mechanisms for authority delegation, policy enforcement, and execution control.
  • 13.4 Experimental Contribution: Developing an experimental environment and measurement scenarios that enable comparisons among different economic action control designs.
  • 13.5 Evaluation Contribution: Developing metrics that help evaluate unauthorized actions, revocation effectiveness, execution integrity, evidence coverage, and outcome verification.

These are intended research contributions. They should not be presented as established contributions before the relevant scientific and experimental work has been completed.

Chapter 14 | Future Research Directions

The CE-AI research program may expand into the following areas:

  • Multi-level economic delegation models.
  • Economic authority management across multiple AI agents.
  • Control of financial actions involving irreversible commitments.
  • Dynamic and time-bound delegation.
  • Verification of economic actions through independent sources.
  • Distributed systems failure management.
  • Prevention of duplicate transactions and unintended side effects.
  • Measurement of the boundaries of out-of-band control.
  • Institutional responsibility models for automated actions.
  • The relationship between technical governance and regulatory compliance.

Each research direction should define its own research questions, variables, and validation methodology rather than expanding the conceptual framework without an experimental justification.

Chapter 15 | Conclusion

The transition from AI systems that generate responses to AI systems that execute actions with economic consequences requires a reconsideration of the relationship between capability, authority, execution, and outcome.

A system's ability to reason, plan, or use tools does not establish that it possesses delegated economic authority. Similarly, granting a system access to a tool does not establish that every action it performs remains within permitted boundaries. Nor does recording a successful operation, by itself, establish that the intended outcome has been achieved.

From this perspective, OUAMARKOM™ proposes the CE-AI™ framework to study and engineer this relationship through an interconnected sequence:

Commands → Delegated Authority → Governed Execution → Verifiable Outcomes

The research program aims to move from conceptual definitions to testable hypotheses, then to engineering architectures justified by evidence, and finally to experimental environments capable of measuring performance and comparing alternative approaches.

The potential research value of this direction does not depend on introducing a new term alone. It depends on its ability to provide precise definitions, reproducible metrics, experimental evidence, and a clear engineering contribution in comparison with existing knowledge and solutions.

«Research earns the right to become architecture. Evidence earns the right to become infrastructure.»
