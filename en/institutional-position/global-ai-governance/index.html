Executive Statement

Executive Statement

Artificial intelligence is moving beyond systems that primarily generate information and recommendations toward increasingly autonomous systems capable of initiating and carrying out actions across digital, commercial, financial, and institutional environments.

This transition creates a fundamental governance challenge: the ability of an AI system to reason, recommend, or initiate an action does not, by itself, establish that the system possesses legitimate authority to execute that action.

As AI agents become more integrated into economic processes, governance must address not only what these systems can do, but also:

  • Who or what grants them authority.
  • The scope and limits of delegated authority.
  • How permissions are evaluated and enforced.
  • How execution can be interrupted, revoked, or restricted.
  • How economic exposure is controlled.
  • How execution outcomes can be independently verified.

OUAMARKOM™ considers this challenge an important research and engineering direction within the broader field of AI governance.

The company's research direction, Economic Engineering of AI, examines the mechanisms through which AI-driven economic actions can be authorized, constrained, executed, and verified.

Within this direction, OUAMARKOM proposes the CE-AI™ - Command Economy of AI framework as a foundational research framework for examining the relationship between commands, delegated authority, governed execution, and verifiable outcomes.

The company's long-term ambition is to contribute to the development of independent engineering infrastructure that supports Governed AI Economic Execution.

OUAMARKOM does not position itself as a global AI policy-making institution, a regulatory authority, or a standards-setting organization. Its proposed contribution is narrower and complementary: researching and engineering technical mechanisms that may help translate governance requirements into enforceable controls over AI-driven economic actions.

Section 1

1. The Global Context: The Evolution of AI Governance

Artificial intelligence governance is becoming an increasingly important international priority.

The rapid development of AI capabilities is creating opportunities across healthcare, education, scientific research, industrial production, financial services, public administration, and economic development.

At the same time, the concentration of advanced AI capabilities, infrastructure, data, and investment among a relatively small number of companies and countries raises questions concerning equitable access, technological sovereignty, accountability, transparency, safety, and the distribution of economic benefits.

International governance initiatives are therefore moving beyond general discussions of AI ethics toward more structured mechanisms for scientific assessment, international cooperation, institutional coordination, and practical governance.

1.1 The United Nations Global AI Governance Framework

The United Nations General Assembly adopted Resolution A/RES/79/325 on 26 August 2025, establishing two distinct but complementary mechanisms:

  1. The Independent International Scientific Panel on AI.
  2. The Global Dialogue on AI Governance.

The Scientific Panel is intended to provide independent, evidence-based assessments of AI capabilities, opportunities, risks, and impacts. Its 40 independent experts, drawn from all five UN regions, were appointed by the General Assembly in February 2026. The Panel published its Preliminary Report on 1 July 2026 and a Thematic Brief on AI agents, misalignment, and the risk of losing human control on 21 September 2026, issued as an advance unedited version.

The Global Dialogue provides an inclusive international platform through which governments and relevant stakeholders can exchange perspectives, discuss governance approaches, and strengthen international cooperation. It is a forum for consultation and cooperation, not a forum for negotiating binding international agreements.

The inaugural Global Dialogue took place in Geneva on 6–7 July 2026, bringing together delegations from 163 countries, together with representatives of industry, academia, civil society, and the technical community. The second annual Dialogue is scheduled for 3–4 May 2027 in New York.

These developments demonstrate the growing institutional importance of international AI governance and the need for stronger connections between scientific evidence, policy discussions, and practical implementation.

Official References:

  • General Assembly Resolution A/RES/79/325.
  • Preliminary Report of the Independent International Scientific Panel on AI, July 2026.
  • Thematic Brief on AI Agents, September 2026 (advance unedited version).
  • Independent International Scientific Panel on AI, FAQ (composition and appointment of the 40 members).

1.2 From Governance Principles to Technical Implementation

A central challenge in AI governance is the transition from high-level principles to mechanisms that can operate within real technical and economic environments.

Principles such as accountability, transparency, human oversight, safety, and responsible deployment require practical implementation mechanisms.

For example:

  • Accountability requires traceable records of decisions and actions.
  • Human oversight requires meaningful intervention and control capabilities.
  • Transparency requires sufficient evidence about how actions were authorized and executed.
  • Safety requires enforceable boundaries rather than policy statements alone.
  • Responsible deployment requires mechanisms for managing permissions, restrictions, and operational exposure.

These requirements raise an important engineering question:

How can governance requirements be translated into technical controls that operate at the point where an AI system's proposed action becomes an actual economic action?

OUAMARKOM identifies this question as a central motivation for its research direction.

Section 2

2. The Research Problem: The Gap Between AI Capability and Economic Authority

AI agents are increasingly being designed to interact with external tools, digital services, enterprise systems, financial applications, and operational environments.

Their capabilities may include analyzing information, selecting actions, initiating workflows, requesting transactions, and interacting with external systems.

However, the existence of these capabilities does not automatically establish the legitimacy or scope of their authority.

A system may be technically capable of initiating a financial transfer without being authorized to transfer the requested amount.

An agent may be capable of purchasing goods without possessing valid procurement authority.

An agent may retain access to an external system even after the underlying authorization has been revoked.

These examples illustrate a distinction between technical capability and delegated economic authority.

2.1 The Capability–Authority Distinction

OUAMARKOM's research begins with the following distinctions:

  • Capability is not Authority. The ability to perform an action does not establish permission to perform it.
  • Authority is not Execution. A valid authorization does not guarantee that the execution process will respect its conditions.
  • Execution is not Outcome. Completing an operation does not establish that the intended economic result was achieved.
  • Evidence is not Verification. The existence of a record does not automatically establish the accuracy, completeness, or independence of the verification process.

These distinctions form an important conceptual foundation for the CE-AI research framework.

2.2 The Economic Execution Governance Gap

OUAMARKOM uses the term Economic Execution Governance Gap to describe the potential absence, insufficiency, or lack of demonstrated coordination among mechanisms that connect:

  1. An AI agent's proposed economic action.
  2. Explicitly delegated authority.
  3. Deterministic policy evaluation.
  4. Controlled transaction execution.
  5. Authorization lifecycle management.
  6. Revocation and intervention mechanisms.
  7. Independently verifiable outcomes.

The existence and practical significance of this gap must be investigated through comparative research, technical experimentation, and measurable evidence.

OUAMARKOM therefore treats it as a research problem rather than as an already established universal conclusion.

2.3 Why This Problem Matters

Without effective mechanisms connecting authority to execution, organizations may face several categories of risk:

  • Unauthorized or excessive economic actions.
  • Execution beyond approved financial or operational limits.
  • Continued execution after authorization has expired or been revoked.
  • Inconsistent enforcement across concurrent requests.
  • Excessive economic exposure resulting from delayed intervention.
  • Insufficient evidence to reconstruct and evaluate execution events.
  • Difficulty distinguishing legitimate agent decisions from actions that exceed delegated authority.

The challenge is not limited to improving the reasoning capabilities of AI models.

It concerns the institutional and technical conditions under which AI-generated decisions are permitted to become real-world actions.

Section 4

4. The Relationship Between Global AI Governance and CE-AI™

The CE-AI™ framework is proposed as a research framework for examining the economic dimension of AI agency.

It focuses on the relationship between commands, delegated authority, governed execution, and verifiable outcomes.

Its foundational sequence is:

Commands → Delegated Authority → Governed Execution → Verifiable Outcomes

4.1 Commands

The command represents an instruction, request, or intended action presented to an AI system or an execution environment.

A command may originate from a human user, an authorized institution, an enterprise workflow, or another permitted system.

The research question is how commands can be translated into explicitly defined and assessable execution requests.

4.2 Delegated Authority

Delegated authority defines the permissions under which an AI agent may act.

It may include:

  • Authorized action categories.
  • Financial or operational limits.
  • Approved counterparties or vendors.
  • Time restrictions and expiration conditions.
  • Contextual and organizational restrictions.
  • Conditions for human approval.
  • Revocation and suspension requirements.

The central research concern is ensuring that authority is explicitly represented and is not inferred merely from the agent's technical capabilities or internal decisions.

4.3 Governed Execution

Governed execution concerns the enforcement of authorization conditions before and during the execution of an economic action.

This may require deterministic policy evaluation, controlled execution interfaces, state management, concurrency controls, and intervention mechanisms.

The objective is to investigate whether execution can be constrained by an authority model that operates independently of the agent's discretionary reasoning.

4.4 Verifiable Outcomes

Verifiable outcomes concern the evidence required to determine what actually happened.

This includes the relationship between:

  • The original command.
  • The delegated authority.
  • The policy decision.
  • The execution event.
  • The resulting state.
  • The evidence used to verify the outcome.

The existence of logs or records alone is not sufficient to establish independent verification. Verification requires appropriate integrity, completeness, traceability, and validation procedures.

4.5 CE-AI as a Complementary Governance Research Framework

CE-AI is not presented as a replacement for international AI governance principles, legal frameworks, or technical standards.

Its proposed contribution is to investigate an engineering dimension that may support the implementation of selected governance requirements in AI-driven economic environments.

The framework remains subject to further research, comparison with existing work, experimental development, and independent assessment.

Section 5

5. Proposed Engineering Contribution

OUAMARKOM's proposed engineering contribution is the development and evaluation of mechanisms that connect delegated economic authority to controlled execution.

Two important directions within this work are the proposed CEP™ - Command Execution Protocol and the Out-of-Band Control Plane architecture.

Their purpose is to investigate how authority enforcement can be separated from the AI agent's own decision-making process.

5.1 CEP™ - Command Execution Protocol

CEP is a proposed engineering specification intended to structure the evaluation and control of AI-generated economic execution requests.

Its conceptual decision model includes three principal outcomes:

  • ADMIT: The request satisfies the applicable authorization and policy conditions.
  • REJECT: The request violates an applicable condition or lacks sufficient authorization.
  • ESCALATE: The request requires additional review, clarification, or human approval.

Potential policy conditions include:

  • Financial limits.
  • Approved vendor or counterparty lists.
  • Authorization scope.
  • Time-to-live (TTL) restrictions.
  • Contextual restrictions.
  • Revocation status.
  • Execution-state requirements.

CEP is currently a proposed engineering direction. It must not be represented as an internationally adopted standard or a fully validated protocol.

5.2 Out-of-Band Control Plane

The Out-of-Band Control Plane is a proposed architectural approach in which authority evaluation and enforcement mechanisms operate outside the agent's ordinary reasoning path.

The research motivation is to reduce the possibility that the same agent whose actions are being governed can independently alter, bypass, or redefine the controls intended to constrain those actions.

The proposed architecture may include:

  • Independent authorization evaluation.
  • Deterministic policy enforcement.
  • Permission and state management.
  • Revocation and emergency intervention.
  • Controlled execution interfaces.
  • Evidence collection and event recording.

Architectural separation alone does not prove security, independence, or resistance to circumvention.

These properties must be established through threat modeling, implementation analysis, adversarial testing, and measurable experimental results.

5.3 Proposed Control Requirements

The engineering research agenda includes investigating:

  1. Whether an authorization decision can be enforced independently of the agent.
  2. Whether revoked authority can be prevented from authorizing subsequent execution.
  3. Whether concurrent requests can be evaluated without violating shared limits.
  4. Whether execution can be interrupted within a measurable time boundary.
  5. Whether economic exposure can be measured and constrained.
  6. Whether execution evidence can be reconstructed and independently assessed.

Any performance targets, including revocation latency or emergency cut-off time, must be treated as engineering targets until demonstrated under documented test conditions.

Section 6

6. Research and Experimental Validation Program

OUAMARKOM proposes a research and experimental program intended to evaluate the feasibility, reliability, and measurable value of governed economic execution mechanisms.

The proposed experimental environment is Smart Hand™, supported by the GEAB experimental scenarios and the UEARM reference implementation direction.

6.1 Smart Hand™

Smart Hand is conceived as a Governed Economic Action Experimental Environment.

Its purpose is to provide a controlled environment for testing the relationship between AI-generated action requests, delegated authority, policy enforcement, execution control, and evidence generation.

Smart Hand should be treated as an experimental environment, not as a completed commercial product or a production-grade infrastructure platform.

6.2 GEAB - Governed Economic Action Benchmarks

GEAB is a proposed experimental benchmark program covering representative economic execution scenarios.

The initial scenario structure includes:

GEAB-01 - Procurement
Examines procurement requests, approved vendors, authorization limits, and policy enforcement.
GEAB-02 - Financial Transfer
Examines transaction authorization, financial limits, execution controls, and economic exposure.
GEAB-03 - Revocation
Examines whether previously delegated authority can be revoked and whether subsequent execution attempts are effectively prevented.

The scenarios are intended to provide structured conditions for comparing execution mechanisms and evaluating their limitations.

6.3 UEARM - Reference Implementation Direction

UEARM is a proposed reference implementation direction intended to integrate relevant governance and execution mechanisms.

Potential components include:

  • Authorization validation.
  • Governance and policy evaluation.
  • Execution state management.
  • Evidence recording.
  • Structured event logs.
  • Experimental reports.

Its implementation and validation status must be documented separately from its conceptual specification.

6.5 Evidence-Based Research Process

OUAMARKOM follows a research process structured as:

Question → Hypothesis → Experiment → Data and Measurement → Engineering Comparison → Decision

This process is intended to ensure that architectural decisions follow evidence rather than precede it.

Section 7

7. Governance, Accountability, and Responsibility

OUAMARKOM considers governance to be a technical, institutional, and organizational responsibility.

The development of mechanisms for controlling AI economic execution must account for the interests of individuals, organizations, institutions, and affected third parties.

The following principles inform the company's proposed approach.

7.1 Human Responsibility

Technical controls should support meaningful human oversight rather than create an assumption that responsibility can be transferred to an automated system.

The delegation of execution authority must remain attributable to an appropriate human or institutional authority.

7.2 Explicit Delegation

Economic permissions should be defined through explicit authorization conditions rather than inferred from the availability of tools or access credentials.

7.3 Least-Privilege Authorization

An AI agent should receive only the authority necessary for its approved task, subject to applicable restrictions and review requirements.

7.4 Revocability

Delegated authority should be capable of suspension, expiration, or revocation through mechanisms that can be evaluated and tested.

7.5 Traceability and Evidence

Economic actions should generate sufficient records to support investigation, accountability, and outcome verification.

7.6 Proportionality

Control mechanisms should be proportionate to the potential financial, operational, legal, and societal impact of the action.

These principles are proposed institutional and engineering commitments. Their practical effectiveness depends on implementation and validation.

OUAMARKOM believes that global AI governance requires contributions from multiple levels of the technology ecosystem.

These include:

  1. Scientific understanding of AI capabilities and risks.
  2. Public policy and institutional governance.
  3. Legal and regulatory frameworks.
  4. Technical standards and interoperability.
  5. Operational controls and implementation mechanisms.
  6. Independent evaluation and verification.

The company's proposed contribution is located primarily at the fifth and sixth levels.

9.1 From Governance Requirements to Enforceable Mechanisms

OUAMARKOM seeks to investigate whether technical infrastructure can support the practical enforcement of governance requirements at the point of economic execution.

Potential contributions include:

  • Explicit economic authority representation.
  • Independent authorization evaluation.
  • Deterministic policy enforcement.
  • Controlled execution mechanisms.
  • Revocation and intervention capabilities.
  • Traceable evidence generation.
  • Measurable outcome verification.

9.2 A Complementary Engineering Layer

The proposed infrastructure is not intended to determine whether an AI system is universally safe, ethical, or compliant.

Those determinations require broader legal, institutional, technical, and contextual assessments.

Instead, the proposed engineering layer focuses on a more specific question:

Given an economic action proposed by an AI agent, can the system establish that the action is authorized, enforce its applicable limits, control its execution, and produce evidence sufficient to assess the outcome?

This is the practical research question OUAMARKOM seeks to investigate.

Section 10

10. Strategic and Economic Implications

The transition toward more autonomous AI systems may create demand for infrastructure that governs how AI agents exercise delegated economic authority.

As agents become capable of interacting with commercial systems, financial services, procurement platforms, and enterprise workflows, organizations may require stronger mechanisms for controlling permissions, execution boundaries, and accountability.

OUAMARKOM's commercial hypothesis is that governed economic execution may develop into a distinct infrastructure requirement within the broader AI ecosystem.

10.1 Commercial Hypothesis

The company intends to investigate whether its research and engineering work could support future infrastructure offerings in areas such as:

  • Governed AI transaction execution.
  • Delegated authority management.
  • Enterprise AI execution control.
  • Economic policy enforcement.
  • Revocation and intervention infrastructure.
  • Execution evidence and verification services.

This represents a strategic commercial hypothesis, not evidence of established market demand, revenue, customer adoption, or product-market fit.

10.2 Potential Value Proposition

The proposed value proposition is not simply to make AI agents more capable.

It is to help organizations establish clearer and more enforceable boundaries between an AI agent's decision-making capabilities and the economic authority it is permitted to exercise.

The value of such infrastructure would ultimately depend on demonstrated improvements in control effectiveness, risk reduction, operational reliability, integration feasibility, and measurable economic value.

Section 13

13. Institutional Governance and Integrity of Claims

OUAMARKOM considers the integrity of its research and public claims essential to its long-term credibility.

The company therefore distinguishes between the following development and evidence statuses:

Development and evidence statuses
StatusMeaning
ProposedA concept or direction has been identified
SpecifiedRequirements or design details have been documented
In DevelopmentImplementation work is underway
ImplementedA working implementation exists
Experimentally DemonstratedResults have been produced under documented test conditions
Independently VerifiedResults have been assessed by an independent party using an identifiable methodology
Institutionally RecognizedA relevant institution has formally recognized or adopted the work

These statuses must not be treated as interchangeable.

In particular:

  • A proposed architecture is not a demonstrated system.
  • A technical specification is not a validated implementation.
  • A successful internal test is not independent verification.
  • A novel concept is not automatically a demonstrated innovation.
  • A research framework is not automatically an established scientific discipline.
  • A public institutional discussion is not an endorsement or adoption of a company's work.

OUAMARKOM will seek to maintain traceability between research claims, supporting evidence, technical artifacts, experiments, and published conclusions.

Section 14

14. Recommendations for the Evolution of Global AI Governance

Based on its research direction, OUAMARKOM identifies several areas that merit further attention in international discussions of AI governance.

These are proposed areas for consideration, not formal recommendations issued by a regulatory or intergovernmental authority.

14.1 Governance of Delegated AI Authority

International AI governance discussions should consider not only model capabilities and outputs, but also the authority delegated to AI agents operating in economic environments.

14.2 Separation of Reasoning and Execution Authority

Research should examine mechanisms that distinguish an AI agent's reasoning process from the authority required to execute external actions.

14.3 Enforceable Human Oversight

Human oversight should be evaluated through practical intervention, suspension, revocation, and recovery mechanisms rather than through the mere presence of human approval procedures.

14.4 Measurable Revocation and Intervention

The ability to revoke permissions and interrupt execution should be assessed through measurable performance and reliability criteria.

14.5 Evidence-Based Economic Execution

AI-driven economic actions should generate evidence sufficient to support traceability, accountability, and outcome assessment.

14.6 Interoperability of Governance Mechanisms

Research should explore interoperable approaches to representing permissions, evaluating policies, controlling execution, and recording evidence across different AI systems and institutional environments.

14.7 Inclusion of Emerging Research and Engineering Contributions

International governance processes should remain open to contributions from universities, independent research groups, emerging technology companies, and institutions outside the largest established AI markets.

This is particularly important for strengthening scientific diversity and broadening participation in the development of practical governance mechanisms.

Section 15

15. Conclusion and Institutional Declaration

The future of artificial intelligence will not be determined solely by how intelligently systems can reason, generate information, or select actions.

It will also depend on how effectively their actions can be authorized, constrained, supervised, interrupted, and verified.

As AI agents become increasingly integrated into economic environments, the distinction between technical capability and legitimate economic authority becomes an important subject for research and engineering.

OUAMARKOM™ seeks to contribute to this field through the research direction of Economic Engineering of AI, the proposed CE-AI™ - Command Economy of AI framework, and the development and evaluation of engineering mechanisms for Governed AI Economic Execution.

The company's proposed contribution is complementary to international policy, scientific assessment, regulatory frameworks, and technical standardization efforts.

Its ambition is to investigate and develop infrastructure that may help translate governance requirements into measurable and enforceable controls over AI-driven economic actions.

OUAMARKOM recognizes that this ambition must be earned through disciplined research, working implementations, reproducible experiments, transparent evidence, and independent evaluation.

Research earns the right to become architecture. Evidence earns the right to become infrastructure.
