1. Research Position

Investigating What Must Hold When AI Can Act Economically

Ouamarkom studies what must hold as artificial intelligence systems move from generating information and recommendations toward participating in actions with real economic consequences.

When an intelligent system can initiate a purchase, transfer funds, modify a price, commit a budget, alter a contract, or execute a sequence of transactions, the question is no longer limited to what the system can do. It also becomes necessary to examine what authorizes the action, which constraints govern it, how execution is enforced, what evidence establishes what happened, and how the resulting economic outcome can be verified and attributed.

Ouamarkom is a research and engineering initiative investigating the conditions and mechanisms that may make AI-mediated economic action governable, enforceable, verifiable, and accountable.

We build the evidence base needed to identify recurring requirements, test potential interventions, and determine whether the findings justify reusable mechanisms, an architecture, or new infrastructure.

We do not assume the required architecture in advance. Nor do we assume that existing mechanisms are insufficient. We investigate both possibilities.

Our central principle: We do not assume the architecture of the AI economy. We build a research method to discover what it may require-and whether it requires anything new at all.

Research Status

  • Stage: Research, Development & Experimental Validation
  • Research subject: Governed Economic Action
  • Method: Controlled experimentation, measurement, comparative evaluation, replication, and evidence verification
  • Intended outputs: Inspectable evidence, reproducible findings, recurring-constraint identification, and evidence-based development decisions
  • Initial experimental base: Saudi Arabia, with broader generalization pursued through replication across systems and contexts

The status and maturity labels used on this page are editorial classifications. They do not establish that a hypothesis has been validated, that a method is an industry standard, or that a particular architecture is necessary.

2. Why Economic Action?

From Producing Intelligence to Studying Action

AI systems are becoming increasingly capable of planning, using tools, interacting with external systems, and coordinating multi-step tasks. These capabilities may expand the range of economic actions that such systems can propose, perform, or mediate.

Technical capability, however, does not resolve the questions of authority, governance, enforcement, and outcomes.

A system may be able to submit a purchase order without being authorized to complete the purchase. It may be authorized to buy goods within a defined budget while a sequence of individually permissible transactions exceeds the aggregate limit. Its authority may be revoked while a previously initiated transaction remains in progress. A transaction may succeed technically while producing an economic outcome different from the intended one.

These are not necessarily manifestations of one underlying problem, and they do not establish that a new technical layer is required. They provide, however, concrete questions that can be investigated experimentally.

Context: Increasing AI Capability

Superintelligence, if realized, could expand the scope of planning, decision-making, and action. It is contextual to this research, not the definition of Ouamarkom, and the research does not depend on the arrival of superintelligence.

Research into intelligence examines the limits of what intelligent systems may be capable of doing. Ouamarkom investigates what must hold when those capabilities are applied to economically consequential action.

Increasing capability does not automatically confer legitimate economic authority, nor does it establish the necessity of a new architecture.

For the conceptual formulation of this question, the paper "The Economic Action Focus" explores whether consequential economic action deserves to be treated as an explicit unit of analysis, with testable propositions and a comparative experimental methodology. It is a conceptual framework; empirical validation is pending.

3. Research Subject: Governed Economic Action

Commands were the original point of entry into this research. The deeper subject is now economic action itself-particularly action performed or mediated by an intelligent system that can produce economic consequences.

Governed Economic Action is used provisionally as a research term for economic action whose applicable authority, governing constraints, execution, evidence, outcome verification, and accountability are examined explicitly.

This definition does not assume that every function must be implemented in one centralized system or organized through a separate protocol. Those remain open architectural and experimental questions.

Questions Organized Around the Action

  1. What is the intended economic action, and what consequences could it produce?
  2. Who or what has the authority to authorize it, and where does that authority originate?
  3. What are the scope, limits, duration, and conditions of the authorization?
  4. Which policies, approvals, and constraints apply?
  5. How is the decision to proceed, reject, or escalate made?
  6. What actually happens during execution, including failures, delays, cancellations, and retries?
  7. What evidence establishes what was authorized, decided, and executed?
  8. How is the economic outcome measured and verified?
  9. How is responsibility attributed for the decision, execution, and outcome?
  10. Which requirements recur across actions, systems, and institutions?
  11. Can existing mechanisms satisfy those requirements?
  12. If not, what is the least complex intervention that measurably improves the outcome?

The Problem Space

Capability→Agency→Authority→Economic Action→Governance and Enforcement→Evidence→Verification→Economic Outcome→Learning and Reassessment.

This sequence is a conceptual map of the research questions, not a completed architecture or a mandatory workflow for every system.

Some stages may overlap or occur concurrently in practice. An economic outcome may occur before it is verified, but it should not be described as a verified outcome until the relevant verification has taken place.

4. Core Research Distinctions

Ouamarkom uses a set of distinctions to separate questions that can otherwise become conflated when evaluating systems capable of taking action.

DistinctionResearch implication
Capability ≠ AuthorityThe ability to perform an action does not establish permission to perform it.
Intelligence ≠ Economic AgencyReasoning performance alone does not establish the ability to participate in economic action.
Intent ≠ AuthorizationExpressing a goal does not grant a system the right to execute it.
Command ≠ PermissionA command expresses an instruction; its validity depends on applicable authority and policy.
Authority ≠ Unrestricted PermissionAuthority may be limited by scope, amount, purpose, duration, or conditions.
Authorization ≠ EnforcementThe existence of permission or a rule does not establish that it is enforced at the point of execution.
Execution ≠ OutcomeExecuting an instruction does not guarantee the intended economic result.
Technical Success ≠ Economic SuccessA technically successful operation may produce an undesirable economic outcome.
Recorded Outcome ≠ Verified OutcomeA recorded result is not, by itself, proof that the result is correct.
Log ≠ Sufficient EvidenceA system log may be incomplete or disconnected from the external economic event.
Evidence ≠ VerificationEvidence must be evaluated to establish what it supports and what remains uncertain.
Learning ≠ AuthorizationLearning from previous actions does not automatically grant additional authority.
Recorded Revocation ≠ Effective RevocationIt is necessary to examine whether revocation reached the point where the economic effect could still be prevented.

These distinctions help define hypotheses and tests. They are research propositions, not universal laws or advance proof that existing systems are inadequate.

5. The Central Research Question

What Must Hold When AI Can Act Economically?

The central question leads to a set of testable inquiries:

  • When are existing identity, permission, and policy mechanisms sufficient?
  • Under what conditions does a gap emerge between authorization and enforcement?
  • Can individually permissible actions accumulate into a violation of an aggregate authority or budget limit?
  • What happens when authority is revoked during execution?
  • How should irreversible actions or actions with delayed economic effects be handled?
  • What evidence is needed to distinguish a submitted command, an executed action, and a verified outcome?
  • Can governance improve without an unjustified increase in false rejection, latency, or operating cost?
  • Which constraints recur across models, architectures, and sectors?
  • Do these constraints require a new intervention, or would better integration of existing mechanisms be sufficient?
  • If a requirement recurs, does it justify a reusable mechanism or a distinct architectural boundary?

These questions define an open research program, not a set of settled conclusions.

The question is not how to build presumed infrastructure. It is which requirement the evidence establishes, what is the least complex intervention that addresses it, and whether that intervention merits becoming a permanent architectural component.

6. Methodology: Evidence Before Architecture

Ouamarkom follows a hypothesis-guided, increasingly inductive approach. We begin with a defined problem, design a test, measure the outcome, and then determine whether further investigation or a different intervention is warranted.

The Research Path

Defined Problem→Research Question→Falsifiable Hypothesis→Controlled Experiment→Data and Evidence→Measurement and Comparison→Replication→Recurring Constraint→Tested Intervention→Potential Architectural Implication→Infrastructure Decision.

Step 1: Define the Action

Select a specific economic action, such as a financial transfer, purchase, or authorization revocation. Define the intended effect and the constraints that apply.

Step 2: Freeze the Hypothesis and Test Conditions

Specify what is being tested, what constitutes success, failure, or an inconclusive result, and which variables must be held constant or changed.

Step 3: Observe Execution

Collect relevant events, including authorization requests, decisions, execution attempts, authority changes, errors, and economic outcomes.

Step 4: Establish and Measure Evidence

Link system records to actual events where possible, calculate predefined metrics, and document the limits of the evidence and sources of uncertainty.

Step 5: Compare and Replicate

Compare outcomes across repeated trials, system configurations, and reasonable alternatives. Seek independent evaluation where feasible.

Step 6: Test the Need for an Intervention

If a recurring limitation emerges, test whether policy changes, better integration, a different enforcement point, or a reusable mechanism improves outcomes.

Step 7: Make the Development Decision

A problem alone does not establish the need for an architecture or infrastructure. The proposed intervention must address a material constraint, and its benefits must justify its cost, complexity, and risks relative to alternatives.

Evidence before architecture. Replication before generalization. Measured improvement before claims of necessity.

The research may conclude that existing mechanisms are sufficient. If reached through sound experimentation, that is a valuable finding.

7. Evidence Maturity and Research Status

This page distinguishes the status of a research work from the maturity of its evidence. A work classified as Current does not necessarily contain a validated hypothesis, and a work classified as Experimental does not necessarily contain observed results.

Work Status

  • Current: Contributes to the present research direction.
  • Foundational: Remains useful for understanding concepts or distinctions, even if its original framing has evolved.
  • Experimental: A hypothesis, method, or model under investigation.
  • Historical: Documents an earlier stage of research development.
  • Superseded: Describes a relationship between an earlier work and a later formulation; it is not a separate maturity level.

Evidence Maturity

LevelMeaning
ConceptualA concept, question, or hypothesis has been formulated.
DesignedAn experiment, metric, or test system has been designed.
ObservedA result has actually been recorded in a documented experiment.
ReplicatedA result has been tested again under stated conditions.
Independently ValidatedThe result has undergone independent evaluation within a defined scope.

No result is presented as observed, replicated, or independently validated unless published evidence supports that classification.

An experiment's design is not evidence of its success. A single positive result is not, by itself, evidence of generalizability.

8. Current Research Dimensions

Ouamarkom organizes its current work around the following dimensions. They are revisable research areas, not a definitive market taxonomy or a final engineering classification.

8.1 Economic Action

Defining the unit, boundaries, and economic consequences of an action, and distinguishing intent, command, execution, and outcome.

8.2 Authority and Delegation

Investigating the source, scope, duration, and conditions of authority, including delegation, restriction, modification, and revocation.

8.3 Governance and Constraints

Examining the policies, approvals, budgets, risk limits, and institutional requirements that determine which actions may proceed.

8.4 Execution and Enforcement

Investigating whether applicable constraints are enforced at the point where an economic effect can occur, including failures, delays, and retries.

8.5 Evidence and Verification

Examining evidence completeness, event-to-record linkage, execution verification, outcome verification, and the limits of what can be established.

8.6 Outcomes and Benchmarking

Developing tests and metrics to compare systems, mechanisms, and alternatives under declared conditions, while separating technical success from economic outcomes.

8.7 Accountability and Learning

Investigating the attribution of decisions, actions, and outcomes, and how prior results can inform reassessment without becoming unauthorized sources of additional authority.

8.8 Economic Action Across Systems

Studying actions that span models, agents, services, external systems, and institutions, including the resulting challenges for authority, evidence, and verification.

8.9 Economic Action Engineering

Exploring whether recurring requirements justify reusable engineering methods or mechanisms, and whether they constitute a distinct engineering direction.

9. CE-AI™: The Research Framework

Command Economy of AI

Command Economy of AI (CE-AI) is a research framework for studying the economic consequences of AI participation in economic activity through commands, delegated authority, governed execution, and verifiable outcomes.

The framework originated in early research into commands as a means of interacting with and directing intelligent systems. As the research evolved, its focus expanded to what happens when commands are connected to consequential economic actions-and what authority, governance, enforcement, evidence, and accountability may be required.

What CE-AI Does Today

CE-AI organizes the questions and relationships investigated by the research program. It does not prescribe the product, architecture, or infrastructure that the research must ultimately produce.

The CE-AI Action Lifecycle

Intent→Command→Authority→Governance→Decision→Execution→Evidence→Verification→Economic Outcome→Learning and Reassessment.

Each stage raises a distinct research question:

  • Intent: What economic objective is being pursued?
  • Command: How is the objective expressed as an instruction or executable specification?
  • Authority: Who or what has the right to authorize the action, and within what scope?
  • Governance: Which policies, constraints, approvals, and institutional requirements apply?
  • Decision: Should the action proceed, be rejected, or be escalated?
  • Execution: What actually happens?
  • Evidence: What records establish the decision, action, and surrounding conditions?
  • Verification: What can be established from the evidence, and to what degree of confidence?
  • Economic Outcome: What economic effect actually results?
  • Learning and Reassessment: What should change in future evaluation, policy, or delegation?

This lifecycle does not imply that every stage must be implemented as a separate technical component or that every system must follow the sequence literally.

What CE-AI Does Not Claim to Be

CE-AI is not:

  • A foundation AI model.
  • An agent product or automation platform.
  • A completed protocol.
  • A finalized architecture or proven infrastructure layer.
  • An industry standard.
  • A validated economic theory.
  • An established market category.
  • A claim that all future economic activity will be command-based.
  • A claim that existing governance systems are insufficient.

CE-AI is the research framework. Governed Economic Action is the deeper subject of investigation.

10. Smart Hand™: The Experimental Environment

Smart Hand™ is a controlled experimental environment for studying, measuring, and reproducing governed economic action.

It enables experiments into how an intelligent system moves from economic intent to decision and execution, and how evidence and outcomes can be examined when constraints are applied, modified, or revoked.

What Smart Hand Investigates

  • Authority and delegation boundaries.
  • Budget constraints and cumulative spending limits.
  • Policy enforcement at the execution point.
  • Responses to authority changes and revocation.
  • Failures, delays, and retries.
  • Evidence completeness and event reconstruction.
  • The relationship between technical execution and economic outcomes.
  • The effects of proposed interventions compared with existing alternatives.

What Smart Hand Does Not Establish by Itself

Its existence does not establish the validity of CE-AI in every application, the industrial validity of GEAB, or the necessity of an institutional gateway or any separate architecture.

The value of the environment depends on the quality of the experiments, measurements, and evidence it produces-not on the tool's existence alone.

Earlier descriptions of Smart Hand as an “execution layer” are preserved in the historical archive because they document an earlier stage of the research. Its current role is experimental.

11. GEAB v0.1: Benchmarking and Measurement

Governed Economic Action Benchmark

GEAB is a proposed research program for developing tests and metrics that compare the performance of systems and mechanisms across governed economic action scenarios.

Its purpose is to turn selected governance and execution questions into repeatable tests with declared conditions, outcomes, and evaluation criteria.

GEAB v0.1 is experimental. It is not an industry standard and does not establish the necessity of any particular architecture.

Illustrative Test Scenarios

Authority Boundaries:

Does the system reject an action that exceeds its authorized scope?

Cumulative Authority:

Can a series of individually permissible actions exceed an aggregate limit?

Revocation:

What happens when authority is withdrawn before execution or while an action is moving through connected systems?

Retries:

Can resubmitting a request produce an unintended duplicate economic action?

Outcome Failure:

Can technical execution succeed while the intended economic outcome fails?

Evidence:

Is the available evidence sufficient to reconstruct what happened and establish what can be verified?

Example: A Cumulative Budget Limit

Assume a delegation permits total spending of 100,000 currency units, with a maximum of 25,000 per transaction.

The system may execute four transactions of 25,000 each. Once the total budget has been exhausted, any additional request must be evaluated against the predefined policy, not merely against the per-transaction limit.

This illustrates a testable hypothesis. It is not a claim of an experimentally observed result.

What Should Be Measured?

Depending on the scenario, relevant metrics may include:

  • Unauthorized action rate.
  • Economic exposure resulting from failure.
  • False rejection rate for permitted actions.
  • Constraint enforcement and revocation latency.
  • Evidence completeness.
  • Outcome verification quality.
  • Unintended duplicate execution.
  • Integration and operating cost.
  • Reproducibility of results.

Each metric must have a defined calculation method and counting conditions before evaluation begins. The report must distinguish what was measured from what was not measured or could not be established.

The Comparative Principle

GEAB should not evaluate a proposed mechanism in isolation. Where feasible, it should compare:

  1. Existing mechanisms under a reasonable configuration.
  2. Existing mechanisms with improved integration.
  3. The proposed intervention or new mechanism.

The question is not whether a proposed mechanism can work. It is whether it improves outcomes relative to alternatives, under which conditions, and at what cost.

13. Architectural Hypotheses: From Requirement to Intervention

Ouamarkom does not begin by assuming the necessity of a gateway, protocol, or infrastructure layer. It begins by asking whether recurring constraints exist that are not adequately addressed by available mechanisms.

If recurring constraints emerge, different interventions can be tested. These may include policy improvements, better integration, changes to delegation management, improved evidence collection, or a distinct architectural boundary.

Institutional Gateway Hypothesis

This hypothesis considers whether a control function or boundary between AI-generated intent or decisions and institutional execution could evaluate the conditions for an action before its economic effect occurs.

The need for a separate boundary is not assumed. The relevant functions may already be available through identity, permissions, policy engines, workflow systems, and financial controls.

Economic Action Gateway Hypothesis

This hypothesis investigates whether certain consequential economic actions require a distinct decision boundary at which authority, scope, risk, approvals, and constraints are evaluated before execution.

The experimental question is:

Does this boundary provide measurable improvement over existing mechanisms or better integration of those mechanisms?

Governed Execution Protocol (CEP)

A proposed protocol could be investigated as a means of expressing, enforcing, and verifying the conditions under which AI systems may execute consequential actions.

CEP remains a development hypothesis, not a completed standard or a component whose necessity has been established.

Any such design should be compared with realistic alternatives, measuring its effects on unauthorized actions, false rejection, latency, evidence quality, and integration and operating costs.

The Potential Architectural Path

Observed Action→Evidence of a Specific Limitation→Recurring Constraint→Tested Intervention→Measured Improvement→Reusable Requirement→Architectural Hypothesis→Infrastructure Assessment.

The process may stop at any stage. Existing mechanisms may be sufficient; improved integration may be enough; the constraint may not recur; or the proposed solution may not justify its cost.

The gateway is a hypothesis. CEP is a hypothesis. Architecture is a hypothesis. Necessity is not assumed; it is tested.

14. Economic Action Engineering

An Emerging Engineering Direction Under Investigation

Ouamarkom investigates whether recurring requirements in governed economic action may give rise to reusable engineering methods and mechanisms.

This direction is provisionally described as Economic Action Engineering.

It examines how consequential economic actions performed or mediated by intelligent systems might be specified, authorized, constrained, governed, executed, evidenced, verified, attributed, and learned from.

Naming this direction does not establish that a distinct discipline already exists. Nor does the presence of similar problems establish the need for a new technical infrastructure.

A stronger case for Economic Action Engineering would require evidence that important, recurring requirements are not adequately addressed by existing practices and engineering methods, and that specific methods or mechanisms improve outcomes in measurable and reproducible ways.

A discipline is not established by declaration. Its scope and necessity must emerge from the problems, evidence, and methods that justify it.

15. Research Decision Principles

Ouamarkom follows a set of principles connecting claims to evidence and engineering decisions to observed constraints.

  • No strong claim without appropriate evidence.
  • No broad generalization without sufficient replication.
  • No architecture without a specific constraint that warrants it.
  • No reusable primitive without a recurring requirement.
  • No infrastructure without a justified operational need.
  • No commercial product without clear customer value.
  • No causal conclusion from a comparison that does not adequately account for relevant factors.
  • Absence of evidence is not automatically evidence of absence.
  • Failure of an individual hypothesis is not necessarily failure of the research program.
  • A sound negative result is not waste; it may prevent unjustified investment.

The Research Investment Rule

Every development decision and expenditure should be tied to a specific uncertainty that the investment is intended to reduce.

Instead of asking, “What should we build next?” we ask:

“Which uncertainty should we resolve next, and what is the least costly experiment that can produce reliable evidence about it?”

These principles guide experiment selection, resource allocation, publication, and development decisions.

19. What Comes Next?

Ouamarkom progresses through evidence-driven research decisions rather than predetermined commitments to a product or architecture.

Research Priorities

  1. Stabilize definitions of actions, authority, outcomes, and verification.
  2. Define experimental scenarios that can be executed in a controlled environment.
  3. Specify metrics and success, failure, and inconclusive criteria before testing.
  4. Run experiments and document raw evidence, results, and limitations.
  5. Compare existing mechanisms with integration improvements and proposed interventions.
  6. Replicate findings across different configurations and seek independent review where feasible.
  7. Publish results, including failures and inconclusive findings.
  8. Determine whether recurring constraints are significant enough to justify a reusable intervention.
  9. Decide whether the evidence warrants a more specific architectural hypothesis.
  10. Assess infrastructure only when evidence establishes a recurring operational need that available alternatives do not adequately address.

What Could Change the Research Direction?

If experiments show that existing mechanisms achieve the required outcomes, that would count as evidence against the need for new infrastructure within the tested scope.

If findings fail to replicate, the hypothesis may need to be narrowed or the measurement approach revised.

If a proposed intervention does not improve outcomes enough to justify its cost, it should not be promoted to a permanent architectural component.

If a recurring constraint, measurable improvement, and reusable requirement emerge, a more specific architectural investigation may be justified.

Research success does not mean proving the original idea right at any cost. It means reaching a reliable conclusion about what should be built, what should not be built, and why.

20. About Ouamarkom

Ouamarkom is a research and engineering initiative investigating the conditions and mechanisms that may make economic actions performed or mediated by AI systems governable, enforceable, verifiable, and accountable.

Ouamarkom uses CE-AI as its research framework, Smart Hand as its experimental environment, GEAB as its developing measurement and benchmarking program, and provisional operational models to turn research questions into testable investigations.

Through experimentation, evidence, replication, and measured interventions, Ouamarkom seeks to identify requirements that recur across actions, systems, and institutions-and determine whether those requirements justify new mechanisms, architectures, or infrastructure.

Ouamarkom does not assume the architectural outcome in advance and does not claim that its current hypotheses have been proven. It builds the evidence base needed to make those decisions.

Canonical Short Description

Ouamarkom builds the evidence base for understanding governed AI economic action, identifying requirements that may justify new mechanisms or architectures, and testing whether those developments are necessary.

Founding Principle

We study the action. We test governance and enforcement. We verify evidence and outcomes. Then we let the evidence determine what should be built.

Research, Development, and Investment

Investment in Ouamarkom is directed toward reducing research uncertainty, developing the experimental environment, refining metrics, producing inspectable evidence, testing findings across different configurations, and pursuing independent validation where feasible.

The intended output is not predetermined to be a gateway, protocol, or standalone infrastructure layer. That decision depends on experimental findings and the value that can be demonstrated for institutions.

Initial Research Base

Initial experiments begin in Saudi Arabia as a base for research, development, and experimental validation. The generalizability of findings is not inferred from location or a single experiment; it must be established through replication across different systems and contexts.

Proposed Institutional Framework

The Provisional Founding Charter (Version 0.1) defines the proposed directions, principles, objectives, and operating framework of the Global Center for Economic Action Research (GCEAR), a proposed research initiative under institutional and research development.

Invitation to Collaborate

Ouamarkom invites researchers, engineers, institutions, and experimental partners to collaborate on repeatable test design, metric evaluation, comparisons between existing mechanisms and proposed alternatives, and independent review of evidence and findings.

Contributions that falsify a hypothesis are as welcome as those that support it.

The open research question remains: What must hold when AI can act economically, and what can be established through experiments about the best way to make that action governable?
